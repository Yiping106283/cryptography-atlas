Overview

Homomorphic encryption

Encryption that lets anyone compute on ciphertexts, producing an encryption of a function of the underlying plaintexts without access to the secret key.

What it is

A homomorphic encryption (HE) scheme adds an evaluation algorithm to public-key encryption. Given an evaluation key, a function f from a class C, and encryptions of m1​,…,mk​, it outputs a ciphertext that decrypts to f(m1​,…,mk​), exactly or, for approximate schemes, within a stated error. Compactness requires the evaluated ciphertext to have size independent of the size of f; without it the problem is trivial. The basic security goal is IND-CPA. Ciphertexts are malleable by design, so HE cannot be IND-CCA2 secure; weaker chosen-ciphertext notions remain possible (). The question was posed as "privacy homomorphisms" () and first answered for all circuits by .

Core notions

  • Partially homomorphic (PHE): one operation, any number of times, e.g. addition in .
  • Somewhat homomorphic (SHE): additions and multiplications up to a bounded degree. Lattice ciphertexts carry noise that grows with each operation and must stay below a threshold for decryption to succeed.
  • Leveled HE: parameters are chosen for a depth bound L fixed at key generation; every circuit of depth at most L can be evaluated ().
  • Fully homomorphic (FHE) and bootstrapping: bootstrapping evaluates the decryption circuit homomorphically on an encryption of the secret key and returns a ciphertext with fresh noise, which allows unbounded depth. Because the encrypted secret key is public, security relies on a circular-security assumption.
  • Noise management: relinearization (key switching) shrinks a ciphertext after multiplication; modulus switching and scale-invariant encodings slow noise growth.
  • Exact versus approximate: BGV and BFV compute exactly modulo a plaintext modulus; CKKS computes on approximate real or complex numbers and treats noise and rounding as bounded precision loss; FHEW and TFHE encrypt bits or small integers and refresh after each gate.
  • Packing (SIMD): one ciphertext holds a vector of slots processed in parallel and rearranged by rotations ().
  • IND-CPA-D: IND-CPA where the adversary also sees decryptions of honestly evaluated ciphertexts. Decrypted CKKS results expose noise and allow key recovery (); exact schemes with noticeable decryption failure can fail it too ().
  • Circuit privacy: the evaluated ciphertext reveals nothing about f beyond its output, even to the key holder; IND-CPA does not imply it. Noise flooding or repeated re-randomization and bootstrapping achieve it ().

Main approaches

  • First generation: ideal lattices () and integers (), with a squashed decryption circuit to make bootstrapping possible.
  • LWE and Ring-LWE with relinearization: SHE from standard LWE (), leveled FHE by modulus switching (), and scale-invariant schemes (, , the basis of BFV). Packing made workloads such as AES evaluation feasible ().
  • GSW and fast gate bootstrapping: approximate-eigenvector ciphertexts multiply without relinearization (). This structure underlies subsecond gate bootstrapping (), TFHE's external product (), and programmable bootstrapping that applies a lookup table while refreshing (, ).
  • Approximate arithmetic: CKKS with rescaling (), bootstrapping by approximate modular reduction (), word-size RNS arithmetic (), and higher-precision refresh ().
